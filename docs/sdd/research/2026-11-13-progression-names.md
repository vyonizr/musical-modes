# Predefined progression names: source-checked theory terms

**Status:** research only. No source code was changed. This note records which established names exist for the six predefined progressions, which current names mislead, and which replacements the sources support. All chords are given in C (Roman numerals relative to the parallel major, as in the app).

**Method and limits.** Theory claims rest on Open Music Theory (OMT, a peer-edited textbook chapter set that cites Biamonte 2010, Tagg 2011, Everett 2009), Ethan Hein's NYU course posts, Wikipedia (only where it cites jazz authors), and Jason Brame's 2011 game-music-theory post. Song checks (question d) are weak: Hooktheory pages for four of the six songs could not be retrieved, so most are marked **unverified**. Where only a chord-site snippet was found, that is stated.

## 1. Melancholic Uplift (`melancholic-uplift`): ♭III–IV–I–I (E♭ F C)

**a. Established name?** None found. OMT's modal and blues schema chapters do not list ♭III–IV–I. Closest named relatives are different progressions: the "triple plagal" ♭III–♭VII–IV–I (Hein's extension of Everett's double plagal; [Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-plagal-cadences/)) and the "Minor Puff" i–♭III–iv ([Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-standard-pop-chord-progressions/)). Neither is this sequence. A Hooktheory-derived list shows ♭III–IV–I occurs in real songs (Blind Faith, "Can't Find My Way Home", post-chorus); that page was seen only as a search snippet, so it supports existence, not a name ([fromthewoodshed](http://fromthewoodshed.com/2011/05/26/pop-chord-progressions/)).

**b. Verdict on current name.** Not an established term. "Melancholic" and "Uplift" are the app's own mood labels; they describe the effect (borrowed ♭III from the parallel minor, resolving to a major tonic) but cite no source.

**c. Candidates.**
1. (iii, neutral descriptive) **♭III–IV–I** (borrowed ♭III into a major tonic).
2. (ii, informal) none found in the sources; the existing mood name is the only community-style label.
3. (i, theory) none; the closest accurate theory phrasing is "modal mixture" (♭III borrowed from the parallel minor), per Hein's note that mixture is the general explanation for such chords ([Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-plagal-cadences/)).

**d. Example song.** *Lift Off*: **contradictory/unverified.** Chordify lists E♭maj7, F, Cm, Dm in C minor ([Chordify](https://chordify.net/chords/lift-off-feat-chino-moreno-and-machine-gun-kelly-official-audio-mike-shinoda-mike-shinoda)), i.e. ♭III–IV–**i**, which fits progression 2, not 1. E-Chords gives the intro as Dm Am C C ([E-Chords](https://www.e-chords.com/chords/mike-shinoda/lift-off)). No Hooktheory analysis was found.

## 2. Melancholic Return (`melancholic-return`): ♭III–IV–i–i in C Dorian (E♭ F Cm)

**a. Established name?** No name for the sequence. Its mode is established: a minor tonic with a **major IV** is the marker of Dorian, and OMT's Dorian schema is the **dorian shuttle** i–IV ([OMT Modal Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)). Every chord (E♭, F, Cm) is diatonic to C Dorian. Note that in this reading the "♭III" is diatonic in Dorian, not borrowed; the numerals are relative to the parallel major only by the app's convention.

**b. Verdict on current name.** Not established; mood label only. The "Dorian" qualifier in the description is accurate and citable (major IV over a minor tonic).

**c. Candidates.**
1. (iii, neutral descriptive) **♭III–IV–i (Dorian)**.
2. (i, theory, partial) "Dorian schema: IV–i motion" (the IV–i cadence of the dorian shuttle). It names the final motion, not the full sequence.
3. (ii, informal) none found.

**d. Example song.** *Glorious* (Arty): **unverified.** Hooktheory's "Glorious" is by The Hoosiers (E major / C♯ minor), a different song ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/the-hoosiers/glorious)). Arty's track was not found in any analysis. (Chordify's reading of *Lift Off* above, E♭–F–Cm, would fit this progression better, but it is low-trust.)

## 3. Mario Cadence (`mario-cadence`): ♭VI–♭VII–I–I (A♭ B♭ C)

**a. Established name?** Yes, three tiers:
- **Aeolian cadence** ♭VI–♭VII–i "(or I)": the textbook name. OMT notes it "very frequently uses a picardy third, which means all the chords are major: ♭VI–♭VII–I" ([OMT Modal Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)). Hein: the term originates in a William Mann article about the Beatles and "isn't technically a cadence" ([Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-standard-pop-chord-progressions/)).
- **Mario cadence**: coined by Jason Brame in a 2011 post ("this progression will be coined the 'Mario Cadence' … until better nomenclature is developed"; he notes it was neither invented nor first used in Mario) ([Brame 2011](http://gamemusictheory.blogspot.com/2011/04/mario-cadence.html)). OMT states "The aeolian cadence has also been called the Mario cadence" ([OMT](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)); Hein calls the major-I ending "the Super Mario Bros ending" ([Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-standard-pop-chord-progressions/)).
- Also "Kondo cadence" (Dan Bruno, 2008, per Brame's links) and "Hollywood cadence": the latter appears only in a forum thread ([vi-control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/help-tracking-source-of-a-few-classic-hollywood-chord-progressions-from-those-knowledgeable-in-gol.34198/)). I found no scholarly use, so it is not recommended.

**b. Verdict on current name.** Established (informal but acknowledged by OMT). Accurate for the major-tonic form. Wikipedia also applies "Mario Cadence" to a "backdoor IV–V" ♭VIM7–♭VII7–I ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backdoor_progression)), consistent with this sequence.

**c. Candidates.**
1. (i, theory) **Aeolian cadence (with Picardy third)**: ♭VI–♭VII–I.
2. (ii, informal) **Mario Cadence** (current).
3. (iii, descriptive) ♭VI–♭VII–I.

**d. Example song.** SMB Overworld Theme: **verified in a reputable analysis.** Brame identifies the SMB Overworld and level-end fanfare as the best-known examples ([Brame 2011](http://gamemusictheory.blogspot.com/2011/04/mario-cadence.html)); OMT and Hein agree on A♭–B♭–C in C.

## 4. Double Plagal (`double-plagal`): ♭VII–IV–I–I (B♭ F C)

**a. Established name?** Yes, exactly. "The 'double-plagal' progression (Walter Everett's term) is an expansion of the plagal progression … to include the IV/IV chord prior to the IV chord … ♭VII–IV–I" ([OMT Blues-Based Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/blues-based-schemas/)). OMT also files it as a Mixolydian schema ([OMT Modal Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)); Hein gives it as B♭–F–C in C and calls it "one of the most familiar things out there" in rock ([Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-plagal-cadences/)). A Groningen soundscapes essay describes ♭VII→IV as a "local" plagal cadence followed by the usual one ([Magnificent flat-seventh](https://www.icce.rug.nl/~soundscapes/VOLUME22/Magnificent_flat-seventh.shtml); search snippet only).

**b. Verdict on current name.** Established; use as is. Hein warns the term "might be alien to rock listeners" even though the sound is not.

**c. Candidates.**
1. (i, theory) **Double plagal** (♭VII–IV–I).
2. (ii, informal) "Mixolydian ♭VII–IV–I" or the descriptive "IV of IV".
3. (iii, descriptive) ♭VII–IV–I.

**d. Example song.** *Some Kind of Love* (The Killers): **unverified.** Only generic chord sites were found; one snippet lists C♯, G♯, E♭, which cannot be reconciled with ♭VII–IV–I without key information ([E-Chords](https://www.e-chords.com/en/chords/the-killers/some-kind-of-love)). Reference examples for the pattern are "Hey Jude" (F E♭ B♭ F) and CCR's "Fortunate Son" ([Hein 2024](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2024/identifying-plagal-cadences/)).

## 5. Backdoor Vamp (`backdoor-vamp`): I–♭VII–IV–I (C B♭ F C), played at half tempo

**a. Established name?** The same four chords are described as the double plagal cadence in the Hey Jude case: Hein writes that "Hey Jude" "uses a chord progression that is formally known as the double plagal cadence: I to ♭VII to IV to I (F to E♭ to B♭ to F)" ([Hein 2022](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2022/mixolydian-mode/)). The tonic-initial form also contains the **subtonic shuttle** I–♭VII ([OMT](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-schemas/)) followed by plagal motion. The sources describe the pattern as a double plagal with an opening tonic; they do not give I–♭VII–IV–I its own name. Which of the two names should dominate is a judgment call.

**b. Verdict on current name.** **Misleading.** In jazz, the "backdoor progression" is iv7–♭VII7–I, or ii–♭VII7–I: a **dominant seventh** on ♭VII (B♭7) standing in for V7 and resolving up to I, with A♭ falling to G ([Wikipedia: Backdoor progression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backdoor_progression) citing Jerry Coker). This vamp uses triads in the order ♭VII→IV→I, so ♭VII never resolves directly to I as a dominant. A jazz musician would not call it a backdoor.

**c. Candidates.**
1. (i, theory) **Double plagal (tonic-initial form)**, with I–♭VII–IV–I stated explicitly.
2. (ii, informal) "Hey Jude progression"/"Hey Jude changes" (descriptive nickname, from the exemplar; I did not find it formalised).
3. (iii, descriptive) **I–♭VII–IV–I (Mixolydian)**.

**d. Example song.** *Walk* (Christoffer Franzén): **unverified.** No chord chart or Hooktheory analysis found for this piano track from the album *Mountain* ([Bandcamp](https://deepelmrecords.bandcamp.com/album/mountain)).

## 6. Haunting Progression (`haunting-progression`): ♭VI–iv–I–I (A♭ Fm C)

**a. Established name?** No name for the full sequence. Its parts are named: **minor plagal cadence** iv–I, described as modal interchange with the parallel minor ([Wikipedia: Plagal cadence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plagal_cadence)), and the related **plagal sigh** IV–iv–I with la–le–sol voice leading, which OMT says evokes nostalgia and sentimentality ([OMT Blues-Based Schemas](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/blues-based-schemas/)). ♭VI (A♭) and iv (Fm) are both borrowed from C minor, so the sequence is a minor-side borrowing resolving to a major tonic. The sources do not cover ♭VI–iv–I.

**b. Verdict on current name.** Not an established name; mood label only. "Haunting" is a perceptual claim without a source.

**c. Candidates.**
1. (i, theory) none for the sequence; closest, **minor plagal cadence** for the iv–I portion.
2. (ii, informal) "plagal sigh" (only IV–iv–I, so it does not match; do not use it as a name).
3. (iii, descriptive) **♭VI–iv–I** (borrowed from parallel minor).

**d. Example song.** *The Worst in Me* (Bad Omens): **plausible, low-confidence.** Chordify lists G, Em, B and Tunebat gives B minor ([Chordify](https://chordify.net/chords/bad-omens-songs/the-worst-in-me-chords), [Tunebat](https://tunebat.com/Info/The-Worst-In-Me-Bad-Omens/2HsYjR4rBa38aePVMWL3zb)). Relative to B as tonic, G = ♭VI, Em = iv and B = I, matching ♭VI–iv–I as a chord set. Chord order, repeat count and key are not confirmed by a reputable analysis; treat as unverified.

| melancholic-return | Melancholic Return | Informal (no established term; "Dorian" qualifier is correct) | **♭III–IV–i (Dorian)** | High on mode (OMT dorian schemas); medium on name |

| double-plagal | Double Plagal | **Established** (Everett; OMT) | **Double Plagal** | High |
| backdoor-vamp | Backdoor Vamp | **Misleading** (jazz backdoor is ♭VII7 → I with a dominant seventh) | **Double Plagal (I–♭VII–IV–I)** or **I–♭VII–IV–I (Mixolydian)** | Medium-high on "misleading"; medium on the replacement |
| haunting-progression | Haunting Progression | Informal (no established term) | **♭VI–iv–I** (descriptive) | High that no standard name exists |

Also decide: progressions 4 and 5 both end on the double plagal idea. If both keep "plagal" in their names, the app should distinguish them by shape (♭VII–IV–I–I versus I–♭VII–IV–I).
